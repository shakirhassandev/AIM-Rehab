Getting your first prosthetic leg is a big step. It's normal to feel excited and nervous at the same time. Many people tell us the worst part is not knowing what will happen.

So here's a plain guide to the process, step by step. Every clinic works a little differently, but the main stages are much the same.

## Before you start: is your limb ready?

After an amputation, your residual limb (the part of your leg that remains) needs time to heal. The swelling also needs to go down. Your doctor or physiotherapist will usually tell you when you're ready to be measured.

During this time, you might be asked to wear a shrinker sock or a compression bandage. This helps your limb settle into a steady shape. It's worth doing, because a limb that's still changing a lot makes the socket harder to fit.

## Step 1: The assessment

Your first visit is mostly talking and checking. We'll ask about:

- Your health and any other conditions, like diabetes
- What your days look like, at home, at work and outside
- What you want to be able to do with your new leg
- Any worries you have

Then we look at your limb. We check the skin, the scar, the shape and how strong your muscles are. We also check your balance and your other leg, since it does a lot of work too.

At the end, we'll explain which kind of leg we think suits you and why. You can ask as many questions as you like.

## Step 2: Casting or measuring

Next, we need the exact shape of your limb. Usually we wrap it in plaster bandage to make a cast. It feels a little cool and damp, and it takes about 15 minutes. It doesn't hurt.

Sometimes we take detailed measurements as well. These help us decide where the socket should hold firmly and where it should give your limb room.

## Step 3: The test socket

This is one of the most important steps. We make a test socket from clear plastic using your cast. When you put it on, we can see exactly where your skin is pressed and where there are gaps.

You might stand in it, take a few steps between parallel bars or simply put weight through it. Tell us how it feels. Your feedback here really matters. Some people need two or three test sockets before the fit is right, and that's normal.

## Step 4: Putting the leg together

Once the socket fits well, we connect it to the other parts: the pylon, the foot and, for above-knee legs, the knee joint.

Then we line everything up while you stand and walk. This is called alignment. Small changes in angle can make a big difference to how natural your walking feels and how much effort it takes.

## Step 5: Learning to use it

Now the real practice begins. We'll show you how to:

- Put on your liner and socks the right way
- Put the leg on and take it off safely
- Stand up, sit down and turn
- Walk on flat ground, then slopes and stairs

You might start with a walking aid like crutches or a frame, and then use less support as you get stronger. A physiotherapist may help with this part too.

## Step 6: Wearing it at home

Don't wear your leg all day straight away. Start with short periods and build up. Check your skin every evening. Red marks that fade within about half an hour are usually fine. Marks that stay, blisters or broken skin mean we need to see you.

Your limb will often shrink a little in the first months. Adding a prosthetic sock is the usual way to keep the fit snug. We'll show you how.

## Step 7: Follow-up visits

Follow-up visits aren't optional extras. They're where a good leg becomes a great one. We check your skin, your walking and the fit, and we make small changes as your body settles.

## A few tips from our team

- Bring a list of questions. It's easy to forget them on the day.
- Bring someone with you if you'd like support.
- Wear shorts or loose trousers that roll up easily.
- Be honest about pain or discomfort. We can't fix what we don't know about.
