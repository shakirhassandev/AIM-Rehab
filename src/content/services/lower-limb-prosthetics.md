## What is a lower limb prosthesis?

A lower limb prosthesis is an artificial leg. It replaces part of a leg that has been lost through an accident, an illness such as diabetes or poor blood flow, or a condition someone was born with.

Every prosthetic leg has three main parts:

- **The socket.** This fits over your residual limb (the part of your leg that remains). It holds the leg on and carries your weight.
- **The pylon or frame.** This is the "bone" of the leg. It connects the socket to the foot.
- **The foot, and a knee joint if needed.** People with an above-knee amputation also need a prosthetic knee.

The socket matters most. If it doesn't fit well, even the best foot or knee won't feel right. That's why we spend most of our time getting the socket shape right.

## Who can a prosthetic leg help?

We see people at different stages. You might be:

- Getting your first leg after a below-knee or above-knee amputation
- Using an old leg that now feels loose, rubs or causes pain
- Changing weight, which has changed the shape of your limb
- A parent whose child has outgrown their prosthetic leg

If you're not sure whether you're ready, come in anyway. We'll check your limb and talk you through the options. There's no pressure to decide on the day.

## Types of prosthetic legs we make

### Below-knee prosthetic legs

This is also called a transtibial prosthesis. Your own knee does the bending, so most people find it easier to learn to walk with this type. We focus on a socket that spreads pressure evenly and keeps the leg secure when you walk, sit and climb stairs.

### Above-knee prosthetic legs

This is also called a transfemoral prosthesis. It includes a prosthetic knee joint. The knee we suggest depends on how active you are, your balance and your daily routine. Some knees lock for extra safety. Others let you walk at different speeds more easily.

### New sockets and repairs

You don't always need a whole new leg. If your current parts are in good shape, a new socket can make a big difference. We also check alignment, which is how the parts line up. Small alignment changes can fix problems like leaning, limping or knee pain on your other leg.

## How the fitting works

We keep the process simple and explain each step. To learn more, read our guide on [what to expect at your first prosthetic leg fitting](/blog/prosthetic-leg-fitting-what-to-expect/).

1. **Assessment.** We look at your limb, skin, strength and balance, and we talk about your goals.
2. **Casting or measuring.** We take a cast or detailed measurements of your limb.
3. **Test socket.** You try a clear test socket so we can see exactly where it presses.
4. **Final leg.** We build your leg and line up the parts while you stand and walk.
5. **Walking practice.** We show you how to put it on, take it off and walk safely.
6. **Follow-up.** We check the fit again once your limb settles.

## Getting used to your new leg

The first weeks take patience. Here's what helps most people:

- Start with short periods and add a little more time each day.
- Check your skin every evening. A mirror helps you see the back of your limb.
- Use prosthetic socks to fine-tune the fit, as your limb can change size during the day.
- Keep your liner and socket clean, and dry them well.

Some soreness is normal at first. Pain that keeps getting worse is not. Our article on [whether prosthetic legs are painful](/blog/are-prosthetic-legs-painful/) explains what's normal and what isn't.

## When to come back and see us

Please contact us if you notice any of these:

- Red marks that don't fade within about 30 minutes of taking the leg off
- Blisters, cuts or broken skin
- A leg that feels loose even with extra socks
- New clicking, squeaking or wobbling
- A big change in your weight or activity level

Small problems are much easier to fix early. You never need to "put up with" a leg that hurts.
